Foundational Heritage

Frank & Maria: The Immigrant Spirit

Long before our family arrived, this soil carried a deep agricultural history. Our estate sits entirely within the 1834 boundaries of Rancho Petaluma, General Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo's 66,000-acre land grant.

In 1911, a 20-year-old immigrant named Frank Flocchini arrived in California from a small village in Lombardia, Italy. Armed with a relentless work ethic, Frank navigated a new country, establishing himself first as a regional share milker. Through rigorous saving, he achieved his dream of purchasing the family's Lakeville farm on April 16, 1919.

Family lore remembers the purchase as a risky gamble. When Frank bought the property, locals openly laughed at the choice, dismissing it as terrible ground where only star thistle could grow. Yet, where others saw a barren paddock, Frank saw a future. He returned to Italy to marry his wife, Maria, and together they returned to the ranch, raised five children, and proved the skeptics wrong. For decades, the property thrived as a traditional family dairy farm, with natural cow manure quietly building a deep layer of organic richness year after year.

Subterranean Preparation

Breaking the Hardpan

When our family prepared to transition the historic pastures to premium vineyard acreage, we knew the land had great viticultural potential, but it required a serious foundational intervention. The Petaluma Gap's intense climate is matched by a dense subterranean clay hardpan.

Before a single length of irrigation pipe was laid or a single trellis row hammered in, we executed an aggressive soil preparation program. Using a massive 6-foot single shank, we deep-ripped the entire estate, tearing the ground in a criss-cross and catty-corner pattern to shatter the hardpan completely. Gypsum was added directly into the cuts to naturally alter the clay structure and unlock the lower mineral strata.

The Living Results: This monumental preparation gave our estate blocks a completely different biological start. When looking at comparable, adjacent vineyards in the area that skipped this step, our competitive advantage is glaringly apparent in vine balance and resilience during hot spells. By permanently altering the hardpan down to six feet, we granted our young root systems an immediate, unrestricted path to dive deep into the earth.

Regenerative Practices

A Decade of No-Till Viticulture: By leaving the soil undisturbed for over a decade, we protect the underground networks of mycorrhizal fungi. This undisturbed soil maintains its natural structural integrity, vastly improving water retention, carbon sequestration, and natural nutrient cycling. This biological stability translates directly into balanced vine canopies and complex flavor profiles.

Herbicide-Free Soil Armoring: We never use synthetic herbicides. Instead, we manage the vineyard floor using a combination of targeted Dorper sheep grazing and mechanical mowing. This lets a protective layer of natural mulch accumulate each season. This armor keeps the soil cool, conserves moisture, and allows microbial life to thrive much later into the dry season compared to bare, exposed dirt.

Irrigation & Water Stewardship: For hydration, we rely entirely on a self-sustaining network of surface-captured rainwater stored on the ranch, protecting local aquifers from depletion.
