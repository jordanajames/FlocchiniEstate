Viticulture From the Ground Up

Our Farming Philosophy & Foundation

At Flocchini Estate, world-class wine is grown physically and biologically from the subsoil up. Rather than forcing synthetic inputs onto the land, we combine deep structural preparation, closed-loop rainwater stewardship, and a living subterranean ecosystem.

Breaking the Clay Hardpan: A Six-Foot Foundation

The Petaluma Gap's marine climate is matched underground by a dense subterranean clay hardpan that restricts root development and water movement if left unaddressed. Before a single length of irrigation pipe was laid, a single trellis post was driven, or a single vine went into the ground, our family executed an intensive, multi-directional soil preparation program.

Pulling a massive 6-foot single shank, we deep-ripped the entire estate footprint—first tearing the ground in a criss-cross grid and following with a diagonal catty-corner pass to shatter the clay pan completely. During this deep structural opening, gypsum was banded directly into the subsurface cuts to permanently alter the clay structure, accelerate drainage, and unlock the lower mineral strata.

Today, the advantage of that early investment is unmistakable. While adjacent vineyards planted on unripped ground struggle against a shallow subterranean wall, our roots dive six feet deep into cooler soil profiles—delivering natural canopy balance, resilience during heat spells, and remarkable flavor concentration.

Deep-rooted Old Wente and Robert Young Chardonnay thriving in deep-ripped Adobe and Goldridge clay.

A Decade of No-Till

Our Stewardship

Over 10 years ago, we committed to a strict no-till approach across our entire acreage. Leaving the soil undisturbed protects the underground networks of mycorrhizal fungi and beneficial microbes that mechanical tilling destroys.

The Winemaker's Advantage

Undisturbed soil retains its natural structural integrity, improving water retention, carbon sequestration, and nutrient cycling—translating directly into balanced canopies and complex fruit.

200 Dorper Sheep

Our Stewardship

To manage under-canopy cover crops, we rotate a resident flock of 200 Dorper hair sheep through the vineyard during dormancy, grazing the rows precisely when needed.

The Winemaker's Advantage

As the flock grazes, they cycle organic nutrients back into the earth, inoculating the topsoil with diverse biology while drastically minimizing heavy tractor mowing passes across the blocks.

100% Herbicide-Free

Our Stewardship

No chemical herbicides, Roundup, glyphosate, or synthetic weed killers ever touch our vine rows.

The Winemaker's Advantage

Cleaner soil yields cleaner, authentic juice. Our fruit enters the winery as an uncompromised, chemical-free canvas ideal for native, wild-ferment programs and low-intervention cellar work.

Surface-Captured Rainwater

Our Stewardship

To protect regional aquifers, we do not draw from local groundwater tables. Our irrigation relies entirely on a self-sustaining network of winter rainwater captured and stored right on the ranch.

The Winemaker's Advantage

We apply targeted deficit hydration only when strictly necessary, encouraging deep root exploration, small berries, and high skin-to-juice ratios.

Our Story • Since April 16, 1919

A Century on the Lakeville Ranch

From General Vallejo's historic 1834 Rancho Petaluma land grant to four generations of Flocchini family stewardship.

Frank & Maria Flocchini: The Immigrant Spirit

Long before our family's arrival, our soil carried a deep agricultural legacy within the historic 1834 boundaries of Rancho Petaluma—General Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo's 66,000-acre land grant. In 1911, a 20-year-old immigrant named Frank Flocchini arrived in California from a small village in Lombardia, Italy. Armed with an unrelenting work ethic, Frank established himself first as a regional share milker before achieving his ultimate dream: purchasing the family's Lakeville ranch on April 16, 1919.

Family lore remembers it as a bold gamble. When Frank bought the property, some locals openly laughed at the choice, dismissing the heavy clay hills as unforgiving ground where only star thistle could grow. Yet where others saw a barren paddock, Frank saw a future. He returned to Italy to marry his wife, Maria, and together they returned to the ranch, raised five children—Madeline (Lena), James, Andrew, Nicholas, and Anna—and proved the skeptics wrong.

For decades, the ranch thrived as a traditional family dairy. Every autumn, natural cow manure was spread over the pastures, quietly building a deep layer of organic richness. That same grit and generational stewardship later inspired brothers Andrew and Nicholas Flocchini to break through the six-foot clay hardpan and establish the estate vineyard blocks that thrive here today.
